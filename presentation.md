## Slide 4 — The problem (Proposed Approach 1/4)

**Title:** A system that grades itself

- Predictive policing sends patrols to where crime was **recorded**.
- More patrol in a district means more of its crime gets recorded.
- Recorded crime is then both the **input** and the **score** of the next decision.
- So the system partly writes its own scorecard: a *self-contaminated proxy*.

**Research question:** What happens when we optimise such a proxy, and is the resulting bias caused by the AI or by the goal?

**Figure:** a simple loop diagram: Patrol → more crime recorded → policy sees and is rewarded on records → more patrol. (Draw it with four boxes and arrows.)

## Slide 5 — Basic approach (Proposed Approach 2/4)

**Title:** A simulated city where the truth is known

- Agent-based simulator of **22 Chicago districts**, built from census data.
- **No historical crime is fed in**, so the true crime in every district is known at every step.
- Policies allocate **20 patrol units** every shift for 140 shifts.
- Each policy is compared with a fair reference: patrol in proportion to **true** crime (the "oracle").

**Figure:** `figures/fig_arch.png`

*Speaker note:* this is what real-world studies cannot do, because in real data nobody knows the true crime.

## Slide 8 — Algorithm (template slide 5, part 1)

**Title:** How one decision works

- Every shift the policy picks **three numbers**: concentration β, hardship tilt w, mixing μ.
- These turn recent recorded crime into patrol shares for the 22 districts (each gets at least 0.5%).
- **Detection:** chance a crime is recorded = 0.30 + 0.55 × min(1, patrol / 1.5).
- **Deterrence:** patrol removes up to 68% of crime (26% per unit of patrol).
- **Reward:** share of *recorded* crime that the patrols covered.
- **Learner:** PPO (Stable-Baselines3), 400,000 steps, learning rate 0.0003, 8 parallel environments.

## Slide 9 — Pseudocode (template slide 5, part 2)

Put this in a monospace text box.

```
TRAIN (naive PPO)
for each training seed in 0..4:
    initialise simulator (22 districts, no crime data injected)
    repeat until 400,000 steps:
        observe last 4 shifts of RECORDED crime (+ district features)
        policy outputs (beta, w, mu)
        shares[r]  = recorded[r]^beta * exp(w * hardship[r])
        shares     = mix with uniform by mu; apply 0.5% floor; normalise
        simulator runs one shift:
            true_crime[r]  = latent crime reduced by deterrence(patrol[r])
            recorded[r]    ~ Binomial(true_crime[r], detect(patrol[r]))
        reward = sum(shares * recorded) / sum(recorded)
        update policy with PPO

EVALUATE
for each of 30 unseen seeds:
    run the policy and the oracle on the SAME seed
    disparity   = patrol of poorest fifth / patrol of richest fifth
    x_oracle    = disparity(policy) / disparity(oracle)
report geometric mean of x_oracle with a 95% bootstrap interval
```

## Slide 10 — Design approach (template slide 6)

**Title:** Design choices and why

| Choice | Why | Drawback | Alternative considered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unseeded simulator | True crime is known, so bias is measurable | Not real-world data | Real crime data (truth unknown) |
| Policy sets 3 rule parameters | Allows exhaustive search and a closed-form model | Less expressive | Free allocation over 22 districts |
| Compare with a need-proportional oracle | Separates "follows crime" from "over-patrols" | The oracle is a fairness norm, not the efficiency optimum | Uniform or parity as reference (also reported) |
| Paired evaluation on the same seeds | Removes simulator noise from comparisons | More computation | Unpaired averages |
| All paper numbers generated by script | The paper cannot drift from the results | Extra tooling | Typing numbers by hand |

**One line at the bottom:** 48 automated tests; a checker verifies every number, table, figure and citation in the paper.

## Slide 11 — Result 1: RL over-patrols poor districts (Demonstration 1/7)

**Title:** E1 — How unfair, and is it at least effective?

- Naive PPO puts **3.9×** the fair reference's disparity on the poorest districts (95% CI 3.0–5.1).
- All 5 training seeds are above the reference (2.6× to 5.3×).
- It covers **less** true crime than the reference: 4.77% against 5.37%.
- PredPol-like rule: 1.41×.

**Figure:** `figures/fig_e1_disparity.png`

*Speaker note:* unfair and not more effective, so the usual "fairness costs accuracy" defence does not apply.

---

## Slide 12 — Result 2: it is the goal, not the AI (Demonstration 2/7)

**Title:** The bias grows with optimisation

- A plain search with **no learning**, maximising the same reward, reaches **13.0×**.
- 39 extra models trained to test this (longer training, four hyperparameter settings).
- Across 20 trained models, more reward goes with more disparity: **rank correlation 0.96**.
- Training 3 times longer (1.2M steps) does not change the picture: 3.9×.
- Best single RL model: 12.0×, almost the same as the search.

**Figure:** `figures/fig_optimisation.png`

*Speaker note:* a better optimiser of this reward is more unfair, not less.

---

## Slide 13 — Result 3: the policy inflates its own score (Demonstration 3/7)

**Title:** E2 and E3 — Contamination

- Recorded coverage overstates true coverage by about **2 percentage points** for PPO (0.3–0.5 for the reference and PredPol).
- The poorest fifth's crime is over-recorded 1.89 times relative to the richest (1.58 for the reference).
- The poorest fifth gets **2.75 times** its share of true crime in patrol (reference: 0.96).

**Figures:** `figures/fig_e2_contamination.png` and `figures/fig_e3_need_residual.png` side by side.

## Slide 14 — Result 4: a maths model explains it (Demonstration 4/7)

**Title:** Why the loop amplifies

- Key quantity: **elasticity ε** = % rise in recorded crime per 1% more patrol.
- Base setting: ε = **+0.20** (detection +0.55, deterrence −0.35).
- Constant detection: ε = **−0.35**, so the loop damps itself.
- A rule's tilt is multiplied by 1 / (1 − β·ε).
- The model matches the full simulator **within 2% on average** (7% at worst).
- The reward keeps rising with concentration after true-crime coverage has peaked.

**Figure:** `figures/fig_theory.png`

---

## Slide 15 — Result 5: switching the loop off (Demonstration 5/7)

**Title:** E5 and sensitivity

- Remove the effect of patrol on detection: PPO's excess falls from **4.9× to 1.5×**.
- Sensitivity: 7 changed settings × 3 seeds. PPO is **3.2× to 9.1×** in every one.
- The size follows the model's elasticity: rank correlation **0.93**.
- Removing socio-economic inputs barely matters (4.4×): the skew comes from recorded crime itself.

**Figures:** `figures/fig_e5_interventions.png` and `figures/fig_sensitivity.png` side by side.

## Slide 16 — Result 6: fixing it (Demonstration 6/7)

**Title:** E4 — Which fairness fix works?

| Method | × fair reference | True-crime coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Naive PPO | 3.9 | 4.77% |
| Parity penalty | far below 1 (overcorrects) | lower |
| Oracle-targeted penalty (w = 0.15) | **0.76** | **4.90%** |

- The parity penalty **under-patrols** the districts with the most crime.
- The oracle-targeted penalty lands closest to fair **and** raises true-crime coverage.

## Slide 18 — Applicability in other domains (template slide 8)

**Common problem:** a system acts, its action changes what gets observed, and it is scored on those observations.

| Domain | The action | The contaminated record | What to change | Data source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recommender systems | What is shown | Clicks happen only on shown items | Replace districts with items; detection = exposure | MovieLens, KuaiRec |
| Healthcare screening | Who is tested | Disease is found only where we test | Districts become patient groups | MIMIC-IV, synthetic cohorts |
| Lending | Who gets a loan | Repayment is seen only for approved applicants | Detection becomes approval | Home Credit, Lending Club |
| Fraud and tax audits | Who is audited | Fraud is found only where audited | Almost direct mapping | Synthetic audit simulators |
| Content moderation | What is reviewed | Violations found only in reviewed content | Districts become communities | Platform transparency reports |

- **What carries over:** the oracle-relative metric, the elasticity model, the targeted penalty.
- **Expected benefit:** tells you in advance whether optimising a metric will amplify bias (is β·ε close to 1?).
- **Limitation:** needs a simulator or some estimate of the ground truth.
- **Compared with existing work:** Ensign et al. (2018) showed the loop for one urn model; we give a measurable, general test.
- **Future scope:** real-data check, richer action space, a second RL algorithm.

## Slide 20 — Conclusion (template slide 10)

- Optimising recorded crime produces unfair patrols: **3.9×** the fair reference, with **less** true crime covered.
- The cause is the **objective**, not RL: a non-learning search is worse (13×), and more reward means more bias (correlation 0.96).
- The loop is the amplifier: switching it off cuts the excess from 4.9× to 1.5×, and a maths model predicts this within 2%.
- A parity penalty overcorrects; a penalty aimed at the fair reference works best.
- **Limits:** simulation only, assumed detection and deterrence values, 22 districts.
- **Target venues:** ACM FAccT, ACM Journal on Responsible Computing, JAAMAS [confirm with guide].

## Likely panel questions (not a slide; for preparation)

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Is this real Chicago crime? | No. The city's structure comes from census data, but no crime data is fed in. That is what lets us know the true crime. |
| Why not use real data? | In real data only recorded crime exists, so the bias we measure cannot be observed. A real-data sanity check is in the pending tasks. |
| Isn't your RL just badly trained? | We tested that. Better-trained models are *more* biased (correlation 0.96), and a search with no learning is the most biased. |
| Where do 0.30 and 0.55 come from? | They are assumptions. We varied them in seven settings and the result held in all (3.2× to 9.1×). |
| What is new compared with Ensign et al. 2018? | Known ground truth in a full simulator, the link between optimisation strength and bias, the elasticity model, and a penalty that targets the fair reference. |
| Why is 1× "fair"? | 1× means patrol in proportion to true crime. We also report results against three other references and two reference-free measures. |
